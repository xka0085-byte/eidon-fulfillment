The First Week After Pet Loss: A Gentle, Practical Checklist

By Eidon, EveryBeloved · October 2026 · 5 min read

The first week after losing a pet is a strange, blurred time. Grief arrives in waves, and somewhere inside those waves there are a handful of practical things that genuinely help — and one that people always wish they'd done sooner. This isn't a grief manual; it's the short list of what actually matters in the first seven days.

1. Save the photos — today

This is the one people regret skipping. Your pet's photos are scattered across phones, cameras, cloud accounts, and other people's phones. In the first days, gather them: the good ones, the blurry ones, the ones where they're doing something only they did. Back them up somewhere that isn't a single phone. If you ever want a keepsake made — or just want to be able to look — this is the week to secure them.

2. Tell someone who gets it

One person who understood your bond is worth ten who say "you can get another one". A friend, a family member, an online community of people who've lost the same species. Disenfranchised grief — grief nobody publicly recognizes — is heavier than the loss itself. Naming it to someone who gets it lightens it.

3. Keep one routine, drop the rest

The walk time, the feeding bowl, the morning greeting — the empty slots are the sharpest part. You don't have to keep the whole routine, but keeping one small anchor (the same morning coffee in the same chair, the same evening walk even without them) holds the week together.

4. Decisions about remains can wait

Scattering, burial, keeping ashes, a garden spot — none of these need to be decided in the first days, and a decision made on day two at 3am is rarely the one you'd make on day ten. If you were cremated and are keeping ashes, they'll keep. If you're considering a keepsake chamber for a pinch of fur or ashes, it ships empty and you fill it when you're ready — there is no deadline on any of this.

5. Do one small ritual

Write down the things only they did. Print one photo. Plant something. Tell the story of how they came to you, out loud, to someone who'll listen. Small rituals are how the loss becomes a memory instead of a wound — and they're entirely yours to design.

6. Consider a keepsake — but only when you're ready

A keepsake can be a comfort or it can be a decision you rush. If you're not sure, wait a week or a month; the pieces will still be here, made to order. If you do know — if you already have the photo you'd want on it — then choosing it early is fine too. There's no correct timeline. The only wrong move is letting someone else tell you it's "too soon" or "too late".

When to get more support

If the grief is stopping you from functioning — can't eat, can't sleep, can't work, for weeks — that's not weakness, that's weight. Many veterinary schools, animal shelters, and pet-loss support organizations run free support lines and groups; your vet can point you to one. Asking for help is part of caring for them, even now.

Wherever you are in this — we're glad you're here, and we're in no rush.
