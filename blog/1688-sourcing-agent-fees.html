What Does a 1688 Sourcing Agent Actually Charge? A Real Cost Breakdown

By Eidon · October 9, 2026 · 8 min read

A 1688 sourcing agent typically charges a service fee of $2–5 per order (or 10–15% of goods value) plus pass-through costs: the goods themselves, domestic courier inside China (¥3–12 per parcel), and international shipping (roughly $3–8 for a typical 100–500 g dropshipping package to the US or Europe). A realistic all-in cost for a $40-retail dropshipping order lands between $18 and $27. Anyone quoting dramatically less is recovering the difference somewhere you can't see — usually inflated shipping or "repacking fees".

That's the direct answer. Below is the full version — every line item, what it actually costs, where agents hide their margin, and a real order worked through end to end. I run a small sourcing and fulfillment operation myself, so these numbers are what I quote, not what I've heard.

The four components of every agent quote

1. Goods cost — the 1688 price, nothing added

1688 is China's domestic wholesale platform, and it is dramatically cheaper than AliExpress — typically 30–60% less for the same item, because you're buying at the factory/trader tier without the retail layer. A phone accessory that costs $6.50 on AliExpress is usually ¥12–20 ($1.70–2.80) on 1688.

A transparent agent passes goods cost through at the invoice price. If an agent won't show you the supplier invoice or claims the goods price is "included in a package deal", you have no way to know whether their goods markup is 10% or 100%.

2. Service fee — where the agent actually earns

Common structures:

  • Flat per-order fee: $2–5 per order. Clean and predictable; best for high-volume, low-value orders.
  • Percentage of goods value: 10–15%. Common for larger or complex orders; scales with effort.
  • "Free" service, margin on shipping: The agent charges nothing for sourcing and earns the spread between their freight contract price and what they quote you. Not dishonest — but ask for the shipping rate card anyway, because "free" makes comparison impossible.

What matters isn't which structure an agent uses — it's whether the total landed cost per order is competitive and stable. Compare agents on that number only.

3. Domestic leg — supplier to consolidation point

Chinese suppliers rarely ship free. Internal courier from supplier to the agent (or consolidation warehouse) runs ¥3–12 per parcel ($0.40–1.70) depending on distance. If you order 3 items from 3 suppliers, that's 3 domestic parcels. This is a real cost — be suspicious of quotes that don't show it.

4. International shipping — the biggest line, and the easiest to hide margin in

Typical direct-line rates for dropshipping parcels (YunExpress-class, full tracking, 7–12 days transit):

WeightTypical range to US/EU
0–100 g$2.50–4.50
100–300 g$4.50–8.00
300–500 g$7.00–12.00
500–1000 g$11.00–18.00

These are ranges, not gospel — they move with fuel surcharges, peak seasons (Q4 adds ¥2–5/kg on many lines), and destination. A good agent quotes you the rate card upfront and passes through carrier adjustments when they happen, in both directions.

Consolidation is the lever most dropshippers ignore: three items from three suppliers shipped separately cost three international fees. Consolidated into one package, you pay one. On a 3-item order this alone often saves $6–12.

Against a $40 retail price, fulfillment is $22.70 — 57% of retail, and that's before ad spend. This is exactly why dropshipping margin math fails when sellers assume "AliExpress price × 3" covers everything: the fulfillment layer is real, and it's the part agents either price honestly or bury in surprises.

Where agents hide margin (the four classics)

  • Inflated shipping: quoting $9 for a shipment their contract prices at $6.50. Only visible if you ask for the rate card.
  • Per-touch fees: "repacking fee", "photo fee", "handling fee" — small individually, $1.50–3 per order in aggregate. I include QC photos and repacking in the service fee; some agents charge per photo.
  • Goods markup: the invoice says ¥120, the quote says ¥150.
  • Currency games: converting at a worse-than-market rate. Ask for quotes in one currency with the rate stated.

None of these make an agent evil — everyone earns somewhere. But you deserve to know where, so you can compare total cost instead of headline cost.
